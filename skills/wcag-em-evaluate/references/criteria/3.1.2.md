# 3.1.2 Language of Parts

Level AA · WCAG 2.0+ · Guideline 3.1 Readable · Group: `structure`

Sources: [Understanding](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/language-of-parts.html) ·
[Spec](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#language-of-parts) ·
[Techniques](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/#language-of-parts) ·
[Plain English](https://aaardvarkaccessibility.com/wcag-plain-english/3-1-2-language-of-parts/) ·
ACT: [de46e4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/act/rules/de46e4/),
[off6ek](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/act/rules/off6ek/)

## Requirement

> The human language of each passage or phrase in the content can be programmatically
> determined except for proper names, technical terms, words of indeterminate language, and
> words or phrases that have become part of the vernacular of the immediately surrounding text.

Terms (WCAG 2.2 glossary): **programmatically determinable**: "determined by software from
author-supplied data provided in a way that different user agents, including assistive
technologies, can extract and present this information to users in different modalities", whose
Example 1 names the mechanism this criterion needs: "Determined in a markup language from elements
and attributes that are accessed directly by commonly available assistive technology" — an
attribute, never italics, a flag icon or quotation marks. **human language**: "language that is
spoken, written or signed (through visual or tactile means) to communicate with humans": codes,
numbers and identifiers are not in a human language. **text**: "sequence of characters that can be
programmatically determined, where the sequence is expressing something in human language" — a run
that expresses nothing in a human language (a part number, a hash, a source-code sample) is not
text and has no language to declare.

The Understanding fixes the unit and the baseline: "Phrases and passages consist of one or more
words. As a result, even an individual word can be subject to the requirements of this criterion,
unless it falls under one of the exceptions", and "When no other language has been specified for a
phrase or passage of text, its human language is the default human language of the web page".

## In plain terms

Mark every passage or phrase whose language differs from the page language with a `lang` on the
element that holds it, so screen readers switch pronunciation rules, braille translators switch
tables and "insert control codes necessary to prevent erroneous creation of Grade 2 braille
contractions", and browsers pick the right script. Unmarked, "the synthesizer will try its best to
speak the words in the default language it works in": the French "voiture" comes out as "voyture".
A single word counts unless it is a proper name, a technical term, of no determinable language, or
adopted into the surrounding language. Text without a `lang` of its own is in the page language
(3.1.1), so a monolingual page needs nothing more. It matters to everyone who listens to the page
or reads it in braille or with text-to-speech.

## Applies when

- Present: `body` or any element under it with a `lang` (`structure.js` → `langParts`: `selector`,
  `lang`, `textSample`, first 50 elements; it queries `body [lang]`, so a `lang` on `body` itself
  is not in it), or any text in a language other than the page language, unmarked: visible text
  (`read`), `option` text in a language select, and names in the tree (`snapshot`: `alt`,
  `aria-label`, `aria-labelledby`, `title`, `placeholder`). Typical sources: switcher links,
  quotations, slogans, untranslated third-party widgets (cookie dialog, chat, payment, map),
  component labels, legal and footer text.
- Not present: neither `body` nor anything under it has a `lang`, and every passage and phrase is
  in the page language or falls under an exception. The `html lang` itself is 3.1.1; its absence
  or wrongness does not change this criterion's outcome.
- Exceptions (normative), judged per run of text:
  - **Proper names** — the Understanding: "a name which is taken to distinctly identify its
    referent – including, but not limited to, a product's brand name or the name of a specific
    person or place". They "can be used across languages without translation (though it may be
    transliterated from its original writing system)" and "generally don't require an explicit
    programmatic change of language": in an English document about philosophy, "the name 'Albert
    Camus' does not need to be explicitly denoted as being in French".
  - **Technical terms** — "Most professions require frequent use of technical terms which may
    originate from a foreign language. Such terms are usually not translated to all languages."
    W3C's own list: "Homo sapiens, Alpha Centauri, hertz, and habeas corpus".
  - **Words of indeterminate language** — a run that is not text in the glossary sense: codes,
    identifiers, placeholder strings, a part number or SKU, a string of digits.
  - **Vernacular of the immediately surrounding text** — "'rendezvous' is a French word that has
    been adopted in English, appears in English dictionaries, and is properly pronounced by English
    screen readers", so an English passage may contain it "without specifying that its human
    language is French and still satisfy this success criterion". Phrases count as much as single
    words: the Understanding's own case is "podcast" twice inside a French sentence, where
    "no indication of language change is required". Two tests, in order: the word is in the
    dictionaries of the surrounding language, and "consider whether the word would be pronounced
    the same (except for accent or intonation) in the language of the immediately surrounding
    text".
  - Presumption for one word: "Frequently, when the human language of text appears to be changing
    for a single word, that word has become part of the language of the surrounding text. Because
    this is so common in some languages, single words should be considered part of the language of
    the surrounding text unless it is clear that a change in language was intended." The
    presumption covers single words only; a phrase of two or more words is judged on the vernacular
    test alone.
- Two Understanding notes that reverse an outcome the procedure would otherwise reach:
  - Marking an exempt word is encouraged and never required: "Though not normatively required to
    satisfy this success criterion, it's a best practice to programmatically indicate the source
    language of these words to encourage accurate pronunciation and improve understandability",
    with W3C's own examples, the second for a different writing system or a transliteration, where
    naming the source language "is especially helpful for accurate pronunciation":

    ```html
    It can be a <span lang="fr">faux pas</span> to omit the <code>lang</code> attribute. Good
    <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">feng shui</span> begins with good markup.
    ```

    Record both a marked exempt word and an unmarked one as a pass.

  - Accessible names are flat strings: "Despite being correctly denoted in the underlying code or
    markup, changes in the natural language of content are not exposed to assistive technologies in
    these cases [...] As these problems are caused by limitations of user agents and assistive
    technologies, and are outside of an author's control, they don't fail the requirements of this
    success criterion." Correct markup inside a name passes even when no screen reader switches
    voice on it.

## axe-core

| Rule         | Decides                                 | Result → outcome                      |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| `valid-lang` | `lang` has a known primary tag (de46e4) | violation → fail (100); pass → step 3 |

Impact Serious. The rule reports failures only, never incomplete, sits in the default WCAG 2.0
Level A & AA rule set, and is recorded as a consistent implementation of de46e4. Settles: on every
state the axe run covered, the validity of every non-empty `lang` and `xml:lang` on `body` and
under it that has non-whitespace text (known primary subtag, case ignored, everything after the
first `-` ignored). Leaves open: whether a valid `lang` names the language of the text it covers
(off6ek); whether passages and phrases in another language carry a `lang` at all (H58; off6ek
"does not check when lang attributes are missing"); which unmarked words and phrases fall under the
four exceptions; names in another language; states axe did not cover and frames; a PDF sample's
Lang entries (PDF19).

A clean axe run never decides this criterion: de46e4's outcome mapping records "All passed
outcomes: success criterion needs further testing" and "An inapplicable outcome: success criterion
needs further testing", so steps 3 to 7 still run. One violation is not automatically an author
defect either: de46e4 assumes "that the lang attribute value is used to indicate the language of a
section of the content. If the lang attribute value is used for something else (for example to
indicate the programming language of a code element), the content may still conform to WCAG despite
failing this rule" — see Deciding.

## Manual procedure

Structure mode: read the sample as loaded, then each recorded state. `snapshot` shows names and
text but never a `lang`; the probe and the eval read the DOM for that. Decide from the markup: an
evaluator "must verify whether or not changes in natural language have been identified correctly
in the underlying code or markup" (Understanding), never from what a screen reader says. H58's own
test procedure is the shape of steps 2 to 4: the value "conforms to BCP 47", "the language code
matches the language of the content it applies to", and "the human language of the content of the
element is the same as the inherited language for the element".

1. Establish the baseline language and inventory every language declaration inside `body`:

   ```bash
   agent-browser wait --load networkidle
   agent-browser eval --stdin < <skill-dir>/scripts/probes/structure.js
   agent-browser get attr body lang
   ```

   Record `lang` (the page language; when it is `null` or wrong, note that for 3.1.1 and use the
   language the page is visibly written in as the baseline), the `lang` of `body` (`langParts`
   queries `body [lang]` and never lists `body` itself) and every `langParts` entry, which stops at
   50 elements and 80 characters: read a short or empty sample in full with `get html "<selector>"`,
   as an empty `textSample` can still be an element whose only text is an image's `alt`. `langParts`
   holds no `xml:lang`; axe judges those, and HTML reads `lang` when the two differ. Where the page
   has more than 50 candidates, or any entry needs its inherited text and word count, run the
   inventory, which builds the ACT notion of "text inheriting its programmatic language" — text
   nodes that are rendered, plus the accessible names of descendants that have no non-empty `lang`
   of their own:

   ```bash
   agent-browser eval --stdin <<'EOF'
   (() => {
     const path = (el) => {
       const parts = []
       for (let e = el; e && e.nodeType === 1; e = e.parentElement) {
         if (e.id) { parts.unshift('#' + e.id); break }
         let s = e.localName
         const sibs = [...(e.parentElement?.children ?? [])].filter((c) => c.localName === s)
         if (sibs.length > 1) s += ':nth-of-type(' + (sibs.indexOf(e) + 1) + ')'
         parts.unshift(s)
       }
       return parts.join(' > ')
     }
     const nameOf = (e) => {
       const by = e.getAttribute('aria-labelledby')
       return (e.getAttribute('aria-label')
         || (by ? by.split(/\s+/).map((id) => document.getElementById(id)?.textContent ?? '')
              .join(' ') : '')
         || e.getAttribute('alt') || e.getAttribute('title') || '').trim()
     }
     const inherited = (el) => {
       const words = []
       const walk = (node) => {
         for (const c of node.childNodes) {
           if (c.nodeType === 3) {
             const t = c.data.trim()
             const shown = node.checkVisibility({ visibilityProperty: true })
             if (t && shown) words.push(...t.split(/\s+/))
             continue
           }
           if (c.nodeType !== 1) continue
           if (c !== el && (c.getAttribute('lang') ?? '') !== '') continue
           const n = nameOf(c)
           if (n) words.push(...n.split(/\s+/))
           walk(c)
         }
       }
       walk(el)
       return words
     }
     const root = document.documentElement
     const all = new Set([root, ...document.querySelectorAll('[lang], [xml\\:lang]')])
     return [...all].map((el) => {
       const lang = el.getAttribute('lang')
       const primary = (lang ?? '').trim().split('-')[0].toLowerCase()
       let intlName
       try {
         const dn = new Intl.DisplayNames(['en'], { type: 'language', fallback: 'none' })
         intlName = dn.of(primary)
       } catch {}
       const words = inherited(el)
       if (el === root) words.push(...document.title.trim().split(/\s+/).filter(Boolean))
       return { selector: path(el), lang, xmlLang: el.getAttribute('xml:lang'), primary,
         intlName: intlName ?? null, inBody: el !== root && !!document.body?.contains(el),
         words: words.length, sample: words.slice(0, 40).join(' ') }
     })
   })()
   EOF
   ```

   `inBody: false` marks the root element, which both ACT rules exclude ("the element is an
   inclusive descendant in the flat tree of a body element"): keep its row as the baseline and
   judge it under 3.1.1. Done when the baseline language and every `lang` and `xml:lang` on `body`
   or under it are recorded with the text each one covers.

2. Confirm that every `lang` value is a known tag (ACT de46e4) on each state the axe run did not
   cover: run `scripts/axe-page.mjs` on that state (SKILL.md) so `valid-lang` decides it, or apply
   the rule by hand. Applicability, all three conditions: the element is an inclusive descendant in
   the flat tree of a `body` element; its document has a content type of `text/html` (an SVG or XML
   document is inapplicable); and "there is some text inheriting its programmatic language from the
   element which is neither empty nor only whitespace" — the inventory's `words: 0` means
   inapplicable, which covers an element whose text is whitespace alone, a lone `&nbsp;` included
   (Inapplicable Example 5). That inherited text is more than visible text nodes: it is text nodes
   that are "visible or included in the accessibility tree", the accessible name **and accessible
   description** of descendants included in the tree, and, for the document element only, the page
   title. Expectation, the rule's only one: "For each test target, the lang attribute value has a
   known primary language tag" — "A language tag has a known primary language tag if its primary
   language subtag exists in the language subtag registry with a Type field whose field-body value
   is language", compared case-insensitively, everything after the first `-` ignored, and neither
   grandfathered tags nor ISO 639.2 three-letter codes count as known. Fails: a language name
   (`dutch`, `english`), a three-letter code for a language that has a two-letter one (`eng`,
   `dut`, `ger`), a grandfathered tag (`i-lux`), symbols (`#!`), whitespace only (`lang="  "`).
   Passes: `EN`, `fr-CH`, `en-US-GB`, `de-hello` (extra subtags are ignored), `zh-Latn-pinyin`,
   `haw` (no two-letter form). Read `intlName` as a hint, not the registry: it names `eng`, `dut`
   and `ger`, which still fail; it leaves registered subtags such as `art`, `mis` and `und`
   unnamed, which still pass; and it throws on a malformed subtag (the `i` of `i-lux`, an empty
   value), which the try/catch turns into `intlName: null`. Where `intlName` and the registry
   disagree, let an `axe-page.mjs` run on that state decide. Hidden content
   does not always remove applicability: a subtree with `display: none` or `visibility: hidden`
   drops out (Inapplicable Example 3), while text that is visible but `aria-hidden="true"` (Failed
   Example 4) and text moved off-screen that is still in the accessibility tree (Failed Example 5)
   both keep their ancestor's `lang` in scope. An element whose whole text sits inside a descendant
   with its own non-empty `lang` is inapplicable and never fails, while that descendant is judged
   on its own value (Passed Example 4, Failed Example 6). An empty `lang=""` is inapplicable: treat
   its text as unmarked in step 4 and as inheriting from the nearest non-empty `lang` above it.
   Done when every entry has a de46e4 outcome.

3. Check that each valid `lang` names the language of what it covers (ACT off6ek, status
   Proposed). Applicability, all four conditions: in `body`, a `text/html` document, "the element's
   lang attribute value has a known primary language tag" (step 2 passed it), and some non-empty,
   non-whitespace text inheriting its programmatic language from it. Expectation, the rule's only
   one: "For each test target, the primary language of its lang attribute value is a most common
   language of the test target", where "The most common language of an element is determined by
   counting the number of words in the text inheriting its programmatic language from this element
   that are part of any of the languages in the language subtag registry. The same word can be part
   of multiple languages. In case of ties, the element has several most common languages. If there
   are no words in the text inheriting its programmatic language from the element, then it has no
   most common language." Apply it to the inventory's `sample` and `words`: the tagged language has
   the highest word count or ties it. A sentence that reads in two languages satisfies either tag —
   "Paul put dire comment on tape" passes with `lang="fr"` and with `lang="en"` (Passed Examples 4
   and 5). A wrapper whose tag matches most of its words passes even when a few words inside it are
   in a third language: those are step 4's question, and words inside a descendant with its own
   `lang` never count for the wrapper (Passed Example 2: a `p lang="nl"` whose English runs all sit
   in `span lang="en"`). Accessible names count toward the wrapper's language and can decide it: a
   `div lang="EN"` holding an English `alt` and a `p lang="FR"` passes (Passed Example 3), while a
   `div lang="fr"` whose image takes an English accessible name from a `hidden` element it points
   at with `aria-labelledby` fails, because that hidden element's own `lang` is ignored and its
   text lands in the image's name (Failed Example 4). Keep step 4 whatever this step returns:
   off6ek "does not check whether a lang attribute should have been used or not. Especially, this
   rule does not check when lang attributes are missing. This must be tested separately and it is
   therefore possible to pass this rule without satisfying Success Criterion 3.1.2 Language of
   Parts." Done when every entry is marked "matches" or "does not match".

4. Find passages and phrases in another language than their effective language that carry no
   `lang` (H58; no ACT rule and no axe rule covers this):

   ```bash
   agent-browser read
   agent-browser snapshot
   ```

   Read the visible text in page order (`read`) for every run whose language differs from its
   effective language — the nearest non-empty `lang` on or above it from step 1, else the page
   language. For each run decide: exempt as a proper name, a technical term, indeterminate, or
   vernacular (the one-word presumption and the pronunciation test under "Applies when"); or
   unmarked. The vernacular test applies to phrases as much as to single words ("fast food",
   "e-mail adres" in Dutch), while only single words carry the presumption of belonging to the
   surrounding language. Sentences, blocks, quotations, untranslated widget labels, `option` text
   in a language select and language names in a switcher are unmarked. Locate each unmarked run and
   read its effective language from the DOM:

   ```bash
   agent-browser eval --stdin <<'EOF'
   (() => {
     const needle = 'Read more'
     const hits = []
     const walker = document.createTreeWalker(document.body, NodeFilter.SHOW_TEXT)
     for (let n; (n = walker.nextNode()); ) {
       if (!n.data.includes(needle)) continue
       const el = n.parentElement
       const owner = el.closest('[lang]:not([lang=""])') ?? document.documentElement
       hits.push({ tag: el.localName, id: el.id, html: el.outerHTML.slice(0, 240),
         owner: owner.localName, effectiveLang: owner.getAttribute('lang') })
     }
     return hits
   })()
   EOF
   ```

   `:not([lang=""])` keeps an empty `lang` from counting as a declaration, matching the ACT
   definition of text inheriting its programmatic language ("F does not have a non-empty lang
   attribute"). Worked example: on a Dutch page (`html lang="nl"`) the cookie dialog reads "We use
   cookies to improve your experience"; the eval returns `effectiveLang: "nl"` and
   `html: '<p class="cc-text">We use cookies…'`, so seven English words sit under a Dutch
   declaration with no `lang` of their own, none of them a name, a technical term or a Dutch loan —
   record it as an unmarked block, selector `.cc-text`, read language English, effective language
   `nl`. Record each unmarked run with its text, selector, effective language and the language you
   read, and each exempt run with the exception that covers it. Done when every run of visible text
   in another language is either exempt with its exception named or listed as unmarked.

5. Check the names and attribute values that are not visible text: `alt`, `aria-label`,
   `aria-labelledby` targets, `title`, `placeholder`, `option` labels and `value` text
   (`snapshot`; `forms.js` → `fields[].name`, `placeholder`, `visibleLabelText`; `images.js` →
   `alt`, `name`, `nameSource`; `links.js` → `name`). A name on an element outside the step 1
   inventory has no `lang` of its own and inherits the page language. Judge the name's language
   against that, then check where the `lang` belongs: content and attribute values can be in
   different languages on one element, and W3C's own example puts the page-language `title` on the
   link and the foreign word in a marked child — `<a title="Spanish" href="…"><span
lang="es">Español</span></a>` on an English page. Do not fail correct markup here for what a
   screen reader does with it: a name flattened from correctly marked children still passes (the
   flat-string note under "Applies when"). Done when every name that is not in the page language is
   listed as unmarked, exempt, or already carried by a `lang` on its own element or an ancestor.

6. Repeat steps 1 to 5 in every recorded state that adds text: open menus (the language switcher
   usually lives there), the cookie dialog, injected widgets, expanded sections, error messages;
   run `snapshot` again after each change (refs go stale). For a frame in scope, take a fresh
   `snapshot -i` and enter it by ref:

   ```bash
   agent-browser snapshot -i
   agent-browser frame @eN
   agent-browser get attr html lang
   agent-browser frame main
   ```

   `frame` scopes `snapshot`, `get text`, `get html` and `get attr`, while `eval --stdin` and
   `read` keep reading the top document, so the probe and the step 4 locator return the wrong
   document inside a frame. The frame's own `html lang` is the baseline for its text (an embedded
   document is not a web page for 3.1.1) and its parts are judged here; when the frame document has
   no non-empty `lang`, de46e4's inheritance definition puts it under its container ("F is a fully
   active document element, has no non-empty lang attribute, and its browsing context container is
   an element inheriting its programmatic language from E"), so its text counts as the parent
   element's language and an English widget in a Dutch page is an unmarked run of step 4. Done when
   every state and every frame in scope has its entries.

7. Record `cannot-tell` for text you cannot assign a language to (an ambiguous script, a
   transliteration, a run you cannot read) and for a PDF sample (PDF19: the browser exposes no
   `/Lang` entries; queue it for a PDF tool). Done when each entry names why, and no entry is left
   without one of pass, fail, exempt or `cannot-tell`.

## Deciding

- Fail (certainty ≥ 90): a non-empty `lang` inside `body` covering text whose primary subtag is not
  a known language tag — a language name (`dutch`, `english`), `eng` or another ISO 639.2 code for
  a language with a two-letter form, a grandfathered `i-lux`, symbols (`#!`), whitespace only
  (`lang="  "`) — (axe `valid-lang` violation: 100; by step 2 on a state axe did not cover: 95),
  except a value naming a programming language, below; an invalid `lang` whose only text is visible
  but `aria-hidden="true"` (de46e4 Failed Example 4) or moved off-screen while still in the
  accessibility tree (Failed Example 5), which stays in scope — 95; a known tag naming a language
  none of the words it covers belong to — `lang="fr"` around a Dutch sentence, a wrapper
  `lang="en"` around the Dutch page body (off6ek) — 90.
- Fail (70–89): a whole block (paragraph, list, quotation, dialog, footer, terms, a widget) in
  another language with no `lang` on it or on an ancestor up to and including `body` — 85; a
  wrapper whose tag matches none of its words while the descendants' tags are wrong as well
  (off6ek Failed Example 2) — 85; a tag contradicted by the accessible name that decides the
  element's most common language (off6ek Failed Example 4) — 85; a phrase of two or more words or a
  label ("Read more", "Powered by", a slogan) unmarked and not adopted into the surrounding
  language — 80; a menu or `select` of language names in their own language without a `lang` per
  link or `option` — 80 (the Understanding's own example); a passage set apart as foreign only
  visually — italics, quotation marks, a flag icon, another font — with no `lang`, so nothing is
  author-supplied for software to read — 80; an `alt`, `aria-label`, `title` or `placeholder` in
  another language on an element without a `lang` — 75; a tag whose language occurs in the text but
  is not its most common language — 75; a single word clearly meant as foreign (quoted, in italics,
  glossed with a translation, in another script) with no `lang` — 70.
- Needs confirmation (50–69): a `lang` on `code`, `pre`, `samp` or a syntax-highlighted block whose
  value names a programming language (`lang="javascript"`, `lang="css"`, `lang="sql"`) — 60, ask
  whether it marks content language or source code, since de46e4 assumes "the content may still
  conform to WCAG despite failing this rule" in that case; a single word that is in the
  dictionaries of the page language but pronounced differently ("meeting", "sale", "service" on a
  Dutch page) — 55, ask whether the author means it as foreign; a name that reads as a phrase (a
  shop called "Little Italy") — 55, ask whether it is a proper name; a word in another script that
  may be a name — 55; `lang` and `xml:lang` disagreeing on one element — 50, ask which is intended
  (HTML uses `lang`).
- Pass: every non-empty `lang` on `body` or under it has a known primary subtag naming a most
  common language of the text inheriting its programmatic language from it — rendered text nodes,
  plus the accessible names and descriptions of descendants without their own `lang` — and every
  passage or phrase in a language other than its effective language is wrapped in an element with a
  `lang` for that language or falls under one of the four exceptions, on every recorded state and
  in every frame in scope.
- Not present: no `lang` on `body` or under it, and all text is in the page language or exempt.
- False positives to avoid: proper names, brands, places ("Albert Camus" in an English text);
  technical terms ("Homo sapiens", "hertz", "habeas corpus"); adopted words and phrases
  ("rendezvous", "podcast", Dutch loans such as "cookies", "download", "fast food"); a word
  pronounced the same in both languages; an exempt word that the author did mark anyway, which the
  Understanding calls "a best practice" and not a requirement (`<span lang="fr">faux pas</span>`,
  `<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">feng shui</span>`); hidden text and empty elements (inapplicable);
  an element whose text is whitespace only, `&nbsp;` included (Inapplicable Example 5); an element
  whose whole text sits in a descendant with its own `lang`, which makes the outer element
  inapplicable (de46e4 Passed Example 4, Failed Example 6); an empty `lang=""` read as an invalid
  value; uppercase or oddly extended values with a known primary subtag (`EN`, `en-US-GB`,
  `de-hello`); a three-letter tag with no two-letter form (`haw`); a bilingual sentence tagged with
  either language ("Paul put dire comment on tape"); a wrapper tag that matches most of its words;
  a `title` attribute in the page language on a link whose foreign content is marked in a child;
  `lang` on `html` (3.1.1); a name flattened from correctly marked children, and a screen reader
  that does not switch voice on correct markup (never a fail).

## Reporting

- File under: 3.1.2. The `html` element's `lang` (missing, unknown, wrong for the page) is 3.1.1;
  an `alt` or accessible name that exists but is in the wrong language is 3.1.2, not 1.1.1 or
  4.1.2. A language switcher without a `lang` per link, an untranslated widget or a template's
  invalid `lang` values is one issue naming every sample it is on; a passage on one page is its own.
- Title patterns: "Language switcher links are not marked with their language", "Cookie dialog in
  English is not marked as English", "Foreign-language passage has no lang attribute", "Quoted
  French passage is marked as Dutch", "Language attribute value is not a valid language code"
- Severity: High when a block a user needs for a task or to understand the page (a dialog, a form,
  terms, the article body) is unmarked or mistagged; Medium when the defect repeats on every page
  (language menu, header and footer labels, a widget) or on several passages of one page; Low when
  one short phrase or accessible name is unmarked and understandable from context. An invalid value
  lands a step higher than its size suggests, because the result is unpredictable rather than
  merely absent: "Some [assistive technologies] will default to the language of the page, whereas
  others will default to the closest ancestor with a valid lang attribute" (de46e4).
- Type: Technical when a template or component needs the `lang` attribute or a corrected value
  (switcher links, widget wrapper, copied template tag); Content when the editor marks the
  passage's language in the CMS, or the phrase or name is translated into the page language.
- Evidence: a screenshot of the passage with `highlight "<css>"` on its element, and the markup
  quoted in the body showing the missing, invalid or wrong `lang` — the `html` field of the step 4
  eval, or `get html` on the parent, since `get html "<css>"` returns inner HTML and not the
  element's own attributes; for the language switcher, the state with the menu open. Quote the
  attribute value exactly as written, case and stray spaces included, because `lang="  "` and
  `lang=""` look the same on screen and only one of them is a failure; name the language you read
  the text as next to the language the markup claims ("text is English, `lang="nl"` is inherited
  from `html`"); for a name-only defect add the `snapshot` line that shows the accessible name. No
  GIF.
- Recommendation: wrap the passage in an element that carries its language as a BCP 47 tag
  ([H58](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/html/H58)): "Use of the primary code is important
  for this technique. Use of sub-codes is optional but may be helpful in certain circumstances", so
  `de` is enough and `de-AT` only adds a variant:

  ```html
  <blockquote lang="de">
    <p>Da dachte der Herr daran, ihn aus dem Futter zu schaffen …</p>
  </blockquote>
  ```

  For a language switcher, mark every link with the language of its own label, which is W3C's own
  example for this criterion:

  ```html
  <ul>
    <li><a href="…" lang="de">Deutsch</a></li>
    <li><a href="…" lang="it">Italiano</a></li>
    <li><a href="…" lang="fr">Français</a></li>
    <li><a href="…" lang="zh-hant">繁體中文</a></li>
  </ul>
  ```

  Where the element's content and its attribute values are in different languages, keep the
  attribute in the page language and mark the content in a child:

  ```html
  <a title="Spanish" href="qa-html-language-declarations-es.html"><span lang="es">Español</span></a>
  ```

  Put the `lang` on the element itself when the foreign text is its accessible name
  (`<button lang="en" aria-label="Close">`), or translate the name into the page language; the
  Understanding allows the first as a workaround for simple cases but warns that "these may not
  work in all user agent/assistive technology combinations". Correct invalid values to the
  registered subtag (`lang="nl"`, not `lang="dutch"` or `lang="dut"`). For a PDF, give the passage
  a `/Lang` entry ([PDF19](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/pdf/PDF19)) — in Adobe Acrobat
  Pro, select the word or phrase, open the Accessibility Tags panel, choose Create Tag From
  Selection, then right-click the new tag and pick the language in the Properties dialog (type the
  ISO 639-2 code for a language that is not in the list); in the file it is a `/Lang` entry on the
  structure element or on a marked-content sequence with a `Span` tag:

  ```text
  /Span << /Lang (es-MX) >>
    BDC
      (Hasta la vista.) Tj
    EMC
  ```

  serves 3.1.1 when the tag wraps the whole document); 1.1.1 and 4.1.2 (a missing name is theirs, a
  wrong-language name is here).
